When you decide your business needs a website, an online store or a custom system, several options show up at once: an independent developer, an agency, a website builder you set up yourself, or the relative who is "good with computers". Any of them can work, and any of them can go wrong.

This guide is not here to push you toward one option. The goal is to give you clear criteria so you can compare them and decide with better information.

## First: do you actually need something custom?

Not always. And that is worth saying.

If you need a page with your services, contact details and some photos, or a store with a regular catalog and standard payments, a platform like Wix, Squarespace or Shopify may be the best answer. It is quick to set up, has a predictable monthly cost and you can change a price without depending on anyone.

Custom development starts to make sense when:

- The way you work does not fit the platform's templates (quotes with their own rules, bookings with conditions, prices per customer or per volume).
- You need the website to talk to other systems: inventory, invoicing, an ERP, messaging apps.
- You want to automate tasks you currently do by hand every day.
- The platform's fees or limits already cost you more than they save.

If none of those sound like you, a platform is probably enough. A good professional will tell you so, even if it means not selling you anything.

## What to ask before hiring

Whoever you are talking to, a few questions will save you a lot of trouble.

- **Can I see real projects that are live?** Not screenshots or loose mockups: links you can open, explore and, ideally, ask the owners about.
- **Who will actually do the work?** At some agencies one person sells the project and someone else builds it, sometimes outsourced. That is not wrong in itself, but you should know.
- **How will we communicate?** Which channel, who your contact is and how quickly they usually reply.
- **How often will I see progress?** Seeing the project grow every week or two is very different from seeing everything at the end, when fixing things costs more.

## What should be in your name

This is often overlooked, and it causes serious problems later. Even if someone else does the work, these things should belong to you:

- **The domain** (yourbusiness.com), registered with your details.
- **The hosting or server**, in your own account or with admin access for you.
- **The source code**, handed over or in a repository you can access.
- **The data**: customers, orders, products. You should be able to export it.
- **The login credentials**, stored by you somewhere safe.

If you stop working with that person tomorrow, you should be able to take everything with you and carry on with someone else without starting over.

## What a good proposal includes

A serious proposal is not a price sent in a chat message. It should explain, in writing:

- **The scope**: what will be built, detailed enough that you both understand it the same way.
- **What is not included**: just as important, because it prevents arguments later.
- **Delivery stages**: what you will receive and in what order.
- **Estimated timelines** for each stage.
- **Payments** tied to those deliveries, not everything up front.
- **What happens after launch**: support, maintenance and how future changes are quoted.

## Warning signs

None of these prove that a project will fail, but if several show up together, it is worth slowing down and asking more.

- There is no previous work you can verify.
- The price or deadline is far better than everything else you have been quoted.
- There is no written scope, only a conversation.
- The domain, hosting and everything else stay in the developer's accounts.
- It is hard to get a reply before you sign (it rarely improves afterward).
- When you ask about support, the answer is "we'll see after launch".

## Why analysis comes before code

Many project problems are not technical: they are things nobody asked about at the start. That is why it pays to begin with a discovery phase: understanding how your business works, who will use the system, which steps repeat and what should happen in the unusual cases.

Take a hypothetical example: a wholesaler asks for "an ordering system". During discovery it turns out each sales rep has their own price list and some customers pay on 30-day terms. If that comes up halfway through development, parts have to be rebuilt. If it comes up first, the system is designed properly from day one.

This stage helps you too: you end up with a clear document you can use to compare quotes, even if you end up hiring someone else.

## After launch

A website or system is not finished the day it goes live. It needs:

- **Security updates** for the language, the framework and its dependencies.
- **Automatic backups**, plus the occasional check that they can actually be restored.
- **Monitoring**, so problems are spotted before your customers notice.
- **Adjustments and improvements** as your business changes.

Ask from the start how all of this is handled and what it costs. Whether it is a monthly plan or billed by the hour depends on the case; what matters is that it is clear.

## Quick checklist

Before you decide, try to have an answer to each of these:

1. Would an off-the-shelf platform solve my problem, or do I really need something custom?
2. Have I seen and used real projects from this person or company?
3. Do I know who will do the work and who I will be talking to?
4. How often will I see progress?
5. Are the domain, hosting, code and data in my name?
6. Do I have a written proposal with scope, exclusions, stages, timelines and payments?
7. Is it clear what happens after launch?
